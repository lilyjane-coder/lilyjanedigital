Grizzly 222

You were right to want a name on this. 222 has a bigger arc than either of us thought — and unlike Grinder, hers isn't finished. Two posts below: her story tomorrow, the general wildfire piece as episode two.

Why the character goes first

The original deck had 222 buried on slide 3 of a wildfire explainer. That was backwards. A character post with a fire hook beats a fire explainer with a character in it — Grinder proved that at 106K.

  • She's a redemption arc, not just a survival story. In 2023 she was relocated for getting into food at a busy picnic area — a food-conditioned bear, the kind whose story usually ends badly. Grinder's story ended in grief. Hers is still going. That's the difference between a spike and a follow.
  • She has an unfinished storyline. Her cubs are old enough to strike out on their own around now. That's a genuine "what happens next" you can come back to — the thing you said you'd already exhausted with Coola.
  • The safety lesson is inside the character. You don't have to bolt "secure your attractants" onto the end. She is the lesson — a bear who got a second chance most don't.

Her actual arc

Everything below is reported. Enough material here for four or five posts, not one.

2023
Relocated with cubs after getting into food at a busy picnic area. Flagged as a bear of management concern.
June 2024
Comes back to her old ground near the Jasper Park Lodge anyway. Bears almost always do.
Jul 24 2024
Winds to 100 km/h drive the largest Jasper fire in a century at the townsite. She takes both cubs to a wet spot by the Athabasca River and stays.
After
Fire burns ~79,000 acres and a third of the town — 358 structures. All three bears walk out.
Late 2024
A few days on the Jasper Park golf course, then high into the Maligne Valley. She never goes back into the burn.
Jun 2025
Staff swap her collar for a new one and ear-tag both cubs.
Now
Cubs at the age where they'd separate from her. Confirm the current status with Parks Canada before you say it on a slide.

She didn't run

Eight slides. Copy is final — lift as written.

1 · hook

When the fire came for Jasper, a grizzly called 222 didn't run.

Her GPS collar recorded exactly what she did instead.

Image

Orange smoke, or a grizzly in profile. Darkest frame you have.

Why

Surprising claim, mechanism withheld. The collar detail promises evidence — that's what makes it feel like a document, not a story.

2 · stakes

July 24, 2024. Winds to 100 km/h. The largest wildfire in Jasper in a hundred years, running straight at the town.

Image

Fire front or heavy smoke over the valley.

3 · the reveal

She walked her two cubs down to a wet spot on the Athabasca River.

And stayed there.

Image

River, ideally with smoke behind it. Quiet frame — this is the held breath.

4 · payoff

79,000

acres burned. A third of the town gone — 358 buildings.

All three bears walked out.

Image

Burn scar, or the sow with cubs. Emotional peak.

5 · the twist

Here's the part people don't know.

The year before, 222 was relocated — for getting into food at a picnic area.

Image

Picnic table, cooler, or garbage bin. Deliberately mundane after slide 4.

Why

Recontextualizes her. She stops being a symbol and becomes a bear with a record — which is what makes slide 6 land.

6 · stakes, again

She came back the next June. They almost always do.

And for a bear that keeps coming back to human food, the next chapter usually isn't a wildfire story. It's a conservation officer.

Image

Empty road at dusk, or a green bin. Not a dead bear.

Careful

Phrased as the general pattern — relocated bears return, food-conditioned bears often end up destroyed. Both well established. Don't claim 222 herself was ever slated for destruction; that isn't reported.

7 · character

After the fire she spent a few days on the golf course, then took the cubs high into the Maligne Valley.

She never went back into the burn.

Image

High alpine, green country. The relief frame.

Why

Gives her judgment. She's not lucky — she made a call. That's what turns an animal into a character people follow.

8 · the ongoing hook

She's still out there

Her cubs are old enough now to leave her and go it alone.

Which means the next hard year is theirs, not hers.

We'll keep following them.

Image

Two young bears, or an empty trail.

Why

A real open loop, not a manufactured one — this is what makes the follow worth something. Verify the dispersal status first.

Post two · +2 to 3 days

The fire isn't what kills them

The general piece, now episode two — with an audience already invested in 222. Same eight-slide shape; only the changed slides are spelled out.

1 · hook

Everyone pictures bears dying in the flames.

That's not how a wildfire kills a bear.

Why

Same assumption-flip engine as your 6K "oversized raccoons" post.

2 · credibility

3%

Researchers pooled 31 studies that tracked collared animals straight through a wildfire. On average, the fire itself killed three percent of them.

Source

Nimmo et al., Global Change Biology 2022. 31 species, 23 families, 1984–2020. Mean predicted mortality 3% (CI 1–9%); severe fires 7%, mild 2%.

3 · callback

You already met one of them.

222 got her cubs out. Then she had to live in what was left.

Why

The callback is the whole point of running the character first — it rewards the people who saw post one and recruits the ones who didn't.

4–5 · the turn

A burned range has no berries, no roots, no cover.

A bear that can't fatten up won't survive hibernation — so it stops looking in the burn and starts looking downhill.

Image

Ash and black trunks, then a bear near a fence line or subdivision edge.

6–7 · the cost

In 2023 — B.C.'s worst fire and drought year on record — conservation officers killed 603 black bears.

That same autumn, one shelter in Smithers took in more than 100 orphaned cubs. The most in 33 years.

Source

CBC/BCCOS: 460 killed in the first nine months of 2023 across 21,000 conflict reports, 603 for the year. Northern Lights Wildlife Society, Smithers: 100+ cubs, peaking above 120.

Careful

Say 2023 out loud. 2025 was a 15-year low — imply kills are peaking now and you'll get corrected in the comments.

8 · the ask

Fires are burning across B.C. right now

The bears will survive the fire.

What they might not survive is what we leave out for them when they come looking.

Lock the bins. Pick the fruit. Feeders down until November.

Why

"A fed bear is a dead bear" stays unsaid. You've spent two posts proving it — let the comments say it for you.

Careful

Check the live fire count the morning you post if you put a number on this slide.

Then the general posting

To your last question — the ratio that works is roughly one character post to two or three general ones.

  • Character posts recruit. They're the spike and the follow. But they burn out fast if that's all you run — nobody follows a soap opera about a bear they can't visit.
  • General posts retain. The moose re-cut, "that's the mistake," the safety evergreens. These prove the account is worth keeping in the feed, and they're what the new followers actually stay for.
  • Come back to 222 when there's news — dispersal confirmed, a sighting, next year's fire season. Not on a schedule. A character post with nothing new to say is how you kill the character.

The bigger play, when you're ready. Grinder belonged to Grouse Mountain. 222 belongs to Parks Canada. Neither is yours — you're borrowing other people's characters, and 222 is an Alberta bear being shown to a BC-heavy audience.

If TrailBear is collecting sightings, the account can mint its own named bear — a local one, followed through user reports. That's a character nobody can take back, it renews itself indefinitely, and following it is a reason to install the app. That's the version of this idea with a moat.
